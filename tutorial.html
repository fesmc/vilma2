Tutorial: the deforming Earth

A conceptual tour of the physics and numerics behind VILMA2

VILMA2 computes how the solid Earth and sea level respond to a changing load of ice and ocean water. This page introduces the ideas behind the model, one at a time, for a reader who is new to glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA) or to this code. Each section gives the physical idea, its central equation and a short note on where it lives in VILMA2. The detailed derivations, parameters and validation are on the linked pages. The page assumes some familiarity with linear algebra and partial differential equations, and nothing about the code.

Refreshers and name origins

Two mathematical ideas recur throughout the model. The first is diagonalization. A linear operator \(L\) is diagonal in a basis of its eigenfunctions \(\phi_k\), since \(L\phi_k = \lambda_k\phi_k\). A field expanded in that basis, \(f = \sum_k c_k\phi_k\), then transforms coefficient by coefficient, \(Lf = \sum_k \lambda_k c_k\phi_k\). A coupled problem thus splits into many small, independent ones. This is the reason spherical harmonics are used horizontally (see below).

The second is fixed-point (Picard) iteration. An equation of the form \(x = F(x)\) is solved by repeating \(x_{k+1} = F(x_k)\) from a first guess. The sequence converges when \(F\) is a contraction, and it needs only evaluations of \(F\), not its Jacobian. The sea-level equation and its coupling to rotation and to the viscoelastic memory are all solved this way.

Several terms carry the names of their originators. The dimensionless response coefficients of a self-gravitating sphere are named after A. E. H. Love, who introduced them in Some Problems of Geodynamics (1911). The horizontal displacement number was added by T. Shida in 1912. The spring-and-damper rheology of the mantle is named after J. C. Maxwell, who proposed it in 1867. The sea-level equation in its modern form goes back to Farrell and Clark (1976).

The mantle as a Maxwell body

On the timescales of glacial cycles the mantle behaves as both a solid and a fluid. It responds elastically to a sudden change in load and flows viscously under a load that persists. A Maxwell body, an elastic spring and a viscous damper in series, captures both regimes with one constitutive law. For the deviatoric stress \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) and strain \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) it reads

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol{\tau}} + \frac{\mu}{\eta}\,\boldsymbol{\tau} \;=\; 2\mu\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}, \]

where \(\mu\) is the shear modulus and \(\eta\) the viscosity. Under a suddenly imposed strain the stress jumps to its elastic value and then decays on the Maxwell time

\[ \tau_M \;=\; \frac{\eta}{\mu}. \]

For the upper mantle of the benchmark earth model M3–L70–V01 (\(\eta = 10^{21}\) Pa s, \(\mu \approx 7\times10^{10}\) Pa), \(\tau_M\) is about 450 yr. A load that changes faster than this sees a largely elastic mantle, while a load held for many Maxwell times sees a fluid one. Glacial cycles span both regimes, so both must be represented in the same simulation.

The real mantle is not a single Maxwell body. It is a stack of layers, each with its own density, shear modulus and viscosity, beneath an elastic lithosphere and above a fluid core. The relaxation of the surface is therefore not set by one Maxwell time. It is set by the whole structure, including the buoyancy of the displaced material, and it typically spans hundreds to many thousands of years. Viscosity may also vary laterally, for example between cratons and tectonically active regions. This lateral structure is the main reason a time-domain method was chosen for VILMA2.

TipIn VILMA2

The layered structure is built in vilma_earth_structure and selected by earth: "M3-L70-V01" (default), "PREM" (density and shear modulus from incompressible PREM on a given layer geometry) or "custom" (the layer arrays r_bot, r_top, rho, mu, eta, rheology). An elastic layer (rheology = 0) is exactly elastic, and a fluid layer (rheology = 2) carries no shear stress. Lateral viscosity is off by default (l_visc_3d = .false.). When it is on, a \(\log_{10}\eta\) field is read from visc_3d_file, by default the Bagge et al. (2021) field of the FesmData store, and clamped to visc_log10_min–visc_log10_max (19.5 to 30). The model does not ship a viscosity file. See Rheology, and the 3-D viscosity input chain for the order in which the field is built.

Spherical harmonics diagonalize the Laplacian

Spherical harmonics are the natural basis for fields on a sphere. Any square-integrable field on the surface can be expanded as

\[ f(\theta,\phi) \;=\; \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} f_{\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi), \]

where the degree \(\ell\) sets the angular scale of a pattern and the order \(m\) distributes its nodal lines between latitude and longitude. Zonal harmonics (\(m = 0\)) vary only with latitude, while sectoral harmonics (\(|m| = \ell\)) vary only with longitude.

The use of this basis follows from one property. The spherical harmonics are the eigenfunctions of the angular part of the Laplacian,

\[ \nabla_1^2\,Y_{\ell m} \;=\; -\,\ell(\ell+1)\,Y_{\ell m}. \]

The Laplacian is thus diagonal in this basis, as described above. For a radially symmetric Earth the field equations of a loaded, self-gravitating sphere (momentum balance, Poisson’s equation and incompressibility) decouple degree by degree. Each degree \(\ell\) reduces to an independent radial problem, and the order \(m\) enters only through the load coefficients. The operator of a degree is therefore the same for all its orders.

In practice the expansion is truncated at a maximum degree \(\ell_\max\). The shortest resolved half-wavelength is about \(\pi a/\ell_\max\), where \(a\) is the radius of the Earth. This gives roughly 310 km at \(\ell_\max = 64\) and 120 km at \(\ell_\max = 170\), the resolution of VILMA1 in CLIMBER-X. The number of coefficients grows as \((\ell_\max+1)^2\), so the cost rises steeply with resolution.

TipIn VILMA2

vilma_sht wraps the SHTns library (Schaeffer 2013) and FFTW for the transforms between the Gauss–Legendre grid and spectral space. The harmonics are real and fully normalized, without the Condon–Shortley phase. The truncation is set by lmax (64 in input/vilma_defaults.nml, while a host must set it itself), and the grid by nlat and nphi (0 gives the de-aliased defaults \(2\ell_\max+2\) and \(4\ell_\max\)). SHTns is initialized without its per-grid algorithm tuning. This is fixed in vilma_sht at compile time and is not a run-time option. See Spectral reduction and Radial finite elements.

Load Love numbers, one response per degree

For a radially symmetric Earth the response to a surface load depends only on the degree of the load, not on its order. A load coefficient \(\sigma_{\ell m}\) therefore produces a vertical displacement, a horizontal displacement and a perturbation of the gravitational potential that are fixed multiples of it. These multiples are the load Love numbers \(h_\ell\), \(l_\ell\) and \(k_\ell\), respectively. Following Farrell (1972) they are defined relative to the potential of the load itself, \(\varphi^L = 4\pi G a\,\sigma/(2\ell+1)\):

\[ h_\ell = \frac{g_0\,U(a)}{\varphi^{L}}, \qquad l_\ell = \frac{g_0\,V(a)}{\varphi^{L}}, \qquad k_\ell = -\frac{F(a)}{\varphi^{L}} - 1, \]

where \(U(a)\) and \(V(a)\) are the radial and horizontal displacement at the surface, \(F(a)\) is the incremental potential there and \(g_0\) is surface gravity. The term \(-1\) in \(k_\ell\) removes the direct potential of the load, so that \(k_\ell\) describes only the deformation.

For a viscoelastic Earth the Love numbers become functions of time. A load applied at \(t = 0\) produces an instantaneous elastic response, which then relaxes toward the fluid limit as the mantle flows. For a homogeneous fluid sphere this limit is known exactly, \(h_\ell \to -(2\ell+1)/3\) and \(k_\ell \to -1\), which makes it a useful test of any GIA code.

Classical normal-mode codes write this time dependence as an elastic term plus a finite sum of exponentially decaying modes (Peltier 1974). The modes are found once per degree, and the response to an arbitrary load history then follows by convolution. This is efficient for a radially symmetric Earth. However, it cannot represent lateral viscosity, which couples the degrees and removes the per-degree response on which the method rests.

VILMA2 therefore does not compute modes. It solves the radial problem of each degree directly in time, following Martinec (2000), so the Love numbers are not inputs to the model. They are diagnostics that emerge from it: the first step gives the elastic response, and a load held long enough gives the fluid limit. The same per-degree solve then serves the 1-D and the 3-D case.

TipIn VILMA2

The radial problem of each degree is discretized with finite elements in vilma_radial_fe, which also holds the Love-number extraction (loading_love, tidal_love). The operator of each degree is factored once by the banded solver in vilma_band and reused at every step. vilma_response wraps the per-degree solves as a response operator, selected by earth_response: "ve" (viscoelastic, default), "elastic" or "null". The elastic \(h_\ell\) of M3–L70–V01 matches the reference table to within 0.08 % up to degree 256 (see Love numbers).

The sea-level equation and its fingerprint

Sea-level change caused by a melting ice sheet is not uniform. The meltwater raises the global mean, but the loss of ice mass also lowers the geoid near the ice sheet and lets the bed rebound there. Relative sea level therefore falls near a shrinking ice sheet and rises by more than the mean far from it. This spatial pattern is the sea-level fingerprint of the ice sheet.

The sea-level equation computes this pattern self-consistently. With the ocean function \(C\) (1 over ocean, 0 over land and grounded ice), the relative sea-level change \(S\) is

\[ S \;=\; C\cdot\bigl(N - u + \Delta\phi\bigr), \]

where \(N\) is the geoid change, \(u\) the vertical displacement of the bed and \(\Delta\phi\) a spatially uniform term. The Earth responds to the total surface load,

\[ L \;=\; \rho_{\text{ice}}\,\Delta I \;+\; \rho_{\text{water}}\,C\cdot S, \]

with \(\Delta I\) the change in grounded ice thickness. The uniform term \(\Delta\phi\) is chosen such that the ocean gains exactly the mass the ice loses. Since \(S\) appears in its own load, the equation is implicit. It is solved by fixed-point iteration: a guess of \(S\) gives the load, the load gives \(N\) and \(u\), and these give an updated \(S\).

Two refinements matter for realistic geometries. First, the coastline migrates. As sea level changes, land floods or emerges and grounded ice may float, so the ocean function itself must be updated. Second, a newly flooded cell does not fill instantly to the full sea-level change, and loading it as if it did puts a spurious step at the shoreline. The first refinement follows Kendall et al. (2005), and the second follows Martinec et al. (2018).

TipIn VILMA2

vilma_sle solves the equation in the pseudo-spectral form of Kendall et al. (2005): the response is applied in spectral space, and the product with the ocean function is formed on the grid. An inner loop iterates \(S\) at a fixed coastline (sle_n_inner = 20, sle_tol = \(10^{-7}\)), and an outer loop migrates the coastline (sle_n_outer = 3). The coastline migrates by default (sle_fixed_ocean = .false.), and newly flooded cells are loaded by their actual water column (sle_subgrid = .true.). Ocean mass is conserved by construction. The densities default to rho_ice = 917 and rho_water = 1000 kg m\(^{-3}\). Against the Martinec et al. (2018) cases D3, E2 and F1 the model agrees to within about 8 % of peak in every field. Case C2 shows an offset that is not yet attributed. See Sea-level equation and its benchmark.

Off-axis mass drives the rotation pole

A redistribution of surface mass changes the inertia tensor of the Earth and thereby moves its rotation axis. Mass placed away from the poles and the equator perturbs the off-diagonal products of inertia, and the pole drifts in response. This true polar wander changes the centrifugal potential, which acts as a further load on the Earth and on the ocean. This feedback has no global mean. Instead, it shifts sea level in a characteristic degree-2, order-1 pattern.

On GIA timescales the Chandler wobble can be neglected. The polar motion \(\mathbf{m}\) then follows the quasi-static linearized Liouville equation (Martinec and Hagedoorn 2014),

\[ \Bigl[\,1 - \tfrac{k^{T}(t)}{k_{s}}\,\Bigr] * \mathbf{m}(t) \;=\; \frac{I(t)}{C-A}, \]

where \(*\) is a convolution in time, \(I\) is the inertia perturbation including the deformation of the Earth, \(k^T\) is the degree-2 tidal Love number, \(k_s\) the secular (fluid-limit) Love number and \(C-A\) the difference between the polar and equatorial moments of inertia. The centrifugal potential of the displaced pole,

\[ \Lambda \;=\; -\Omega^{2}a^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,(m_1\cos\phi + m_2\sin\phi), \]

is a degree-2, order-1 field. It enters the sea-level equation as one more contribution to the sea surface, so no separate solver is needed.

TipIn VILMA2

vilma_rotation carries two degree-2 viscoelastic channels (one forced by the load, one by the centrifugal potential) on the same radial operator as the load response. The rotation and the sea level are iterated to a common fixed point inside vilma_sle. Rotation is on by default (l_rotation = .true.) and is switched off for the non-rotating community benchmarks. rotation_k_s = 0 (default) uses the model’s own fluid limit for \(k_s\), and rotation_c_minus_a sets \(C-A\) (default \(2.63\times10^{35}\) kg m\(^2\)). The polar motion matches Spada test 3/2 to within 0.9 %. See Rotation and its benchmark.

Explicit memory and time stepping

VILMA2 advances the Maxwell rheology explicitly in time. The total stress at a step is the elastic stress of the current strain plus a memory stress \(\boldsymbol{\tau}^V\) that carries the relaxation history. In the default scheme it is updated as

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}^{V,i} \;=\; (1-M)\,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{V,i-1} \;-\; 2\mu M\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\,i}, \qquad M \;=\; \frac{\mu\,\Delta t}{\eta}, \]

where \(i\) is the step index and \(M\) is the Maxwell number of the step (Martinec 2000). The memory stress enters only the right-hand side of the field equations. The left-hand operator is therefore independent of time, of the time step and of viscosity. It is factored once per degree, and each step costs a back-substitution.

This choice has two consequences. First, lateral viscosity changes only the scalar \(M\), which multiplies known fields pointwise. The memory advance is then done on the grid and transformed back to spectral space, while the solve stays decoupled by degree. Second, the scheme is only conditionally stable. The step must satisfy \(\Delta t < 2\eta_{\min}/\mu\), so the lowest viscosity present sets the step. At the default floor of \(10^{19.5}\) Pa s and \(\mu \approx 7\times10^{10}\) Pa the limit \(M \le 1\) is about 15 yr. The coupling interval of a host model is thus split into shorter sub-steps, with the ice load interpolated linearly across the interval.

A second, implicit scheme is available. The trapezoidal (Crank–Nicolson) update is second-order accurate and A-stable, so its step is limited by accuracy rather than stability. Its step size is chosen adaptively, by comparing one full step with two half steps. Since the end-of-step strain depends on the end-of-step memory, each step is iterated together with the sea-level equation.

TipIn VILMA2

The memory scheme is selected by scheme: "fe" (explicit, default), with equal sub-steps at \(M \le\) cfl (default 1), or "trap" (trapezoidal, adaptive), with the tolerances rtol = \(10^{-4}\) and atol = \(10^{-3}\). The sub-step controller is vilma_timestep, the per-degree Maxwell kernel is in vilma_viscoelastic, and the field driver is in vilma_response. A host advances the model with solid_earth_update(se, h_ice, dt_yr) from vilma_coupling, which passes the coupling interval in years. With lateral viscosity the toroidal field is carried by default (l_toroidal = .true.). A deglaciation at \(\ell_\max = 170\) with 3-D viscosity then takes about 2.7 min of wall time per kyr on 64 threads (see Toroidal flow). A restarted run reproduces an uninterrupted one bit for bit. See Rheology and Time stepping.
